02

Participatory research

Who helps make the research decisions?

Participatory research brings people with direct experience of a community, issue, or condition onto the research team. They work alongside people with formal research training and take part in decisions about the questions, methods, interpretation, and use of the work.

03

Participatory action research

Research becomes part of changing the conditions being studied

Participatory research becomes participatory action research when the inquiry helps people address a problem, fill a gap, transform an institution, or take another form of collective action. Sometimes the group acts on the findings. Sometimes change begins through the research process itself.

What can improve

People with firsthand knowledge can help the group ask more useful questions, build conditions for honest participation, and notice context that outsiders may miss.

What must still be examined

Participation does not erase power. A PAR collective still needs to ask who controls resources, whose knowledge is treated as credible, who carries risk, and who benefits from the work.

04

A readable PAR practice map

Assumptions, commitments, and methods

María Elena Torre’s PAR Map pairs each assumption with commitments a collective can make and practices it can use. The original one-page chart is dense, so the same relationships are unfolded below for reading and presentation.

1 People hold valuable knowledge about their lives and experiences

Commitments

  • Value knowledge that institutions have historically marginalized or delegitimized.
  • Value traditionally recognized knowledge, including scholarly knowledge.
  • Share knowledge and resources so collective members can participate as equally as possible.

Methods and practices

Offer qualitative and quantitative research training. Build knowledge with youth, elders, historians, statisticians, judges, and other people relevant to the inquiry.

2 People can develop strong critical analyses

Commitment

Decide the questions, research design, methods, analysis, and useful products collaboratively.

Methods and practices

Develop concepts and theory through rounds of rough drafts that the group creates and recreates. Use discussion and theory-of-change exercises.

3 People hold multiple identities, histories, connections, and responsibilities

Commitments

  • Create space for people to express their multiplicity and use it to inform the research.
  • Make room for creative risk, recognizing that people and ideas remain under construction.

Methods and practices

Use methods that allow more than one kind of response, such as open surveys, identity mapping, focus groups, and graffiti museums. Vary activities so different members can contribute in different ways.

4 People and institutions sit within systems shaped by power and privilege

Commitments

  • Examine power relationships inside the collective and throughout the research.
  • Explore disagreement rather than smoothing it over for consensus.
  • Work with power inside the group in ways that support individual and collective needs.

Methods and practices

Re-member bodies, knowledge, and histories of resistance that have been excluded. Seek outlier cases and counter-stories that reveal how margins are created and where damage, resistance, privilege, and possibility appear.

5 Knowledge production is not objective or value-free

Commitments

Think through the consequences of research and action. Attend to power and vulnerability within the collective and created by the research.

Methods and practices

Create more than one research product and seek audiences that will respect and engage the findings.

6 Social research benefits from multiple methods

Commitment

Choose approaches suited to the questions and remain honest about what different kinds of data can and cannot establish.

Methods and practices

Possible methods include surveys, interviews, focus groups, archival data, maps, oral histories, photovoice, participant observation, and cross-site visits.

7 Participation and change require ongoing work

Commitments

Keep negotiating the conditions of collaboration and building research relationships over time. Understand action across multiple levels and moments, within one project and across connected efforts.

Methods and practices

Plan meaningful ways for people to enter, participate, and exit. Possible products and actions include articles, presentations, feedback sessions, symposia, performance, data postcards, and campaigns.

Reformatted from María Elena Torre’s Participatory Action Research Map (2009). The source chart remains the authority.

05

Questions worth asking

Use these throughout the PAR process

Who should be part of the research collective?

What mix of shared experience and purposeful difference does the inquiry need?

What conditions would support meaningful participation?

How might collective members and allies use the data in different ways?

How does the project connect to ongoing struggles for social justice?

Who owns the data?

In whose voices will the work be written, performed, or published?

How will the collective represent both shared purpose and meaningful difference?

What is the purpose of the research?

Whom does the research seek to reach, educate, or move to action?

How might the research be used or misused?

Who could be made vulnerable by the research, and what protections are needed?

What is the audience being asked to do?

What could shift collective responsibility into collective action?

Where does the collective want to help create change?

Bring this into your Growth Journal

Choose a problem that affects UC Berkeley students. How would your first description change after people with direct experience begin shaping the inquiry?
